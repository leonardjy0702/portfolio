Case Study
Lenovo Qira – Pay Attention

Introduction

Pay Attention is a core feature inside Lenovo Qira — the personal ambient AI that launched at CES 2026. It enables real-time transcription, translation, and AI-generated meeting summaries so that users stay fully present in conversations, without the cognitive overhead of note-taking or the frustration of language barriers.

Company Lenovo – AI Ecosystems Group
Project Date September 2025 – January 2026
Role Lead UX Designer
Platform Windows · Android · Cross-device

Project Summary

Lenovo Qira is a system-level personal AI that spans Lenovo PCs, tablets, and Motorola smartphones. Rather than living inside a single app, Qira is embedded directly into the OS — accessible via a dedicated key, the wake word "Hey Qira," or a persistent interface element. Pay Attention is one of Qira's flagship experiences: when enabled, it listens to meetings and conversations, captures live transcriptions and translations, flags key decisions and action items, and generates summaries users can revisit, edit, and share.

The product had a clear predecessor problem: Lenovo AI Now captured meeting audio in a way that felt opaque and privacy-invasive to testers, and the output was a raw wall of text with no structure. The opportunity was to redesign this from first principles — making AI-assisted meeting capture feel genuinely useful, trustworthy, and ambient.

The Challenge

Several research workshops and a cross-functional kick-off surfaced the core problem space. Users needed a meeting tool that could capture what mattered — without requiring them to manage it. The key problems to solve:

  • How do we make AI meeting capture feel safe to turn on — especially in professional and multi-participant settings?
  • How do we surface decisions and action items from a raw transcript without false positives?
  • How do we support non-native English speakers who need translation in-context, without disrupting meeting flow?
  • How do we design a recall experience that is faster and more trustworthy than human memory?
  • How do we integrate this into the OS-level Qira experience rather than building another siloed meeting app?

Research & Discovery

Three primary research methods were used to build a foundation of user insight before any design work began.

  • Contextual inquiry (n=8): observed participants in live hybrid and remote meetings, documenting moments of distraction, language confusion, and note-taking interruptions.
  • Diary study (n=14, 2 weeks): participants logged every moment they wished they had a better record of a conversation. Entries were coded into four themes: missed decision, language barrier, distraction, and loss of context over time.
  • Competitive audit: reviewed Otter.ai, Microsoft Copilot transcription, Google Meet captions, and Zoom AI Companion — assessing transcript accuracy, privacy model, recall UX, and integration depth.
67% of professionals leave meetings without clear next steps (Deloitte Workplace Survey, 2025)
2.3× more likely to miss a key decision when participating in a second language (user interviews, n=22)
41 min average time lost per week chasing colleagues for meeting recap (internal diary study)
3 in 5 users had previously disabled an AI recording feature due to privacy concerns about always-on listening

Core insight: Users don't want an AI that records everything — they want one that captures the right things with explicit consent, and surfaces them in context when it matters. The difference between surveillance and support is control.

The Solution

Working alongside the product owner, engineering lead, and Qira's design system team, I designed and iterated the Pay Attention experience across three parallel streams: the live transcript layer, the post-meeting summary flow, and the real-time translation mode. Key design decisions and their rationale:

Chosen

Persistent transcript strip

A thin live-text bar at the screen edge — visible but non-intrusive. Users can expand to full view on demand. Keeps eyes on the meeting, not a modal.

Rejected

Pop-over transcript modal

Interrupted meeting flow in Round 1 testing. Users found themselves managing the window rather than listening. Abandoned after Round 1.

Chosen

Opt-in per session

Activation is per-meeting, never persistent. A consent notification is surfaced automatically to all call participants — not just the activating user.

Chosen

Inline translation callouts

Contextual translation notes for idioms and jargon appear inline rather than in a hidden tap-to-expand panel — increasing discoverability for multilingual users.

Usability Testing

Two rounds of moderated usability testing were conducted: Round 1 used lo-fi Figma prototypes with 8 participants; Round 2 used a functional Qira build on a ThinkPad with 6 participants across the US, Germany, Japan, India, and Brazil. Each session included simulated meeting scenarios with a 20-minute recording task.

  • Critical
    Consent notification unclear

    5 of 8 users in Round 1 didn't know whether others in the call could see that recording was active. The fix: a system-level toast now notifies all meeting participants automatically — not just the activating user. Re-tested in Round 2: 78% rated privacy controls as "clear" or "very clear."

  • Medium
    Action items vs. decisions confused

    Users couldn't reliably distinguish between the two AI tag types in the live transcript. Solution: separated into distinct colors (red chip for decisions, blue chip for action items) and added the owning person's name directly on the action item chip. Confusion dropped significantly in Round 2.

  • Medium
    Translation note feature undiscoverable

    Multilingual users in Round 1 missed the contextual translation notes entirely — they were hidden behind a tap-to-expand interaction. Moved to a persistent inline callout for idiom-heavy terms, with a tap-to-dismiss for users who don't need it. All Round 2 multilingual participants noticed and used the feature.

  • Positive
    Summary accuracy praised

    All 6 Round 2 participants rated the AI summary as "accurate" or "mostly accurate." A key driver: users trusted the output significantly more when they could see an inline supporting transcript excerpt linked directly to each summary point.

Outcomes

Pay Attention shipped as part of Lenovo Qira at CES 2026 on select Lenovo AI PCs, with a rollout to Motorola smartphones to follow. Post-launch metrics from early access users:

4.3 out of 5 · meeting recall satisfaction
78% rated privacy controls "clear" or "very clear"
2.1× faster recall vs. manual notes in user testing
91% task success rate across core flows in Round 2

Conclusion

As the lead UX designer on Pay Attention, I worked end-to-end — from research and persona development through prototyping, testing, and delivery into Qira's design system. I partnered with the product owner to prioritize features against business goals, embedded with the scrum team to guide daily implementation, and ran two rounds of testing to validate and iterate the design before ship.

What I'd do differently

The biggest lesson from this project was that privacy UX is inseparable from trust in the core feature. We initially treated consent as a legal checkbox — after one round of testing, it became the most critical design problem we had to solve. If I were starting again, I'd run a dedicated privacy mental models study in the first week of research, before any wireframing.

I'm also proud of how the translation contextual notes evolved. What started as a secondary feature for bilingual users became one of the most praised moments in all of testing — a reminder that designing deeply for one persona often creates unexpected value for everyone.
